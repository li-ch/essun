Furthermore, distance-aware congestion management is a critical requirement in Ultra Ethernet (UE) to 
accommodate the growing diversity of traffic patterns across modern network deployments. Storage 
traffic is frequently mirrored across multiple datacenters, often spanning distances of thousands of 
kilometers. The replication process requires congestion management techniques that account for 
latency and bandwidth constraints associated with mixed short- and long-distance transmission. 
Similarly, AI and high-performance computing (HPC) systems increasingly operate across distributed 
datacenter infrastructures [55]. These architectures generate network flows that traverse both short 
intra-datacenter paths and long inter-datacenter links [56]. To optimize performance and minimize 
congestion, endpoints are required to differentiate between short-distance and long-distance flows. 
This distinction enables adaptive congestion control mechanisms that adjust transmission behavior 
according to the network distance, ensuring efficient data transfer across varying deployment scenarios. 
By integrating distance-aware congestion management, UE can address the specific needs of storage 
replication and AI/HPC workloads while maintaining network stability and performance. 
While baseline algorithms such as NSCC and RCCC provide strong defaults, they are fixed. Evolving site 
conditions create a need for forward-compatible mechanisms. Operators may need a way to adopt new 
methods, swap in alternative algorithms, or apply targeted modifications for the new conditions. 
PCM addresses these gaps by providing a programmable way to extend and adapt congestion policies in 
the network. PCM provides configurability to network administrators. This approach enables fine-
grained control over congestion management policies, allowing adjustments to optimize network 
performance in response to specific deployment requirements and evolving traffic conditions. 
PCM offers functionality similar to the congestion control mechanisms implemented in the QUIC 
protocol [54]. However, unlike QUIC, where congestion control operations are managed in software at 
the transport layer, PCM enables designers to offload these functions entirely to the network hardware 
at the endpoint. This hardware-based approach ensures low-latency congestion management, reduces 
computational overhead on hosts, and enables highly efficient and scalable control mechanisms that 
operate seamlessly within high-performance networking environments. 
3.6.18.1 PCM Architecture 
The UE PCM architecture is designed as a logically structured framework composed of four distinct 
components: 
1. Incoming signals: Signals that gather information about the current state of the network as well 
as relevant operational state from the endpoint, form the first component. These signals 
provide data necessary for congestion analysis and management. 
2. Congestion control (CC) algorithm: The algorithm, including its state, that processes the 
collected signals to compute appropriate control actions. By interpreting network conditions 
and endpoint states, the algorithm determines precise adjustments required to optimize data 
flow and mitigate congestion. The CC algorithm defines congestion control. 
3. Load balancing (LB) algorithm: The algorithm that assigns entropy values to transmitted request 
packets and reacts to network signals when responses for transmitted requests are received.
