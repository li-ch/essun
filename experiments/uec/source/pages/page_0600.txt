4.2 Congestion Signaling (CSIG) 
Contemporary machine learning (ML) training and high-performance computing (HPC) workloads 
compute across a switched network fabric in closely coupled, synchronous fashion. As a result, they 
demand short flow completion times, even at the tail of the distribution. For these workloads, overall 
application progress can stall when even a single flow’s completion is delayed. 
When a flow completes, it abruptly releases the capacity it had previously consumed at links along its 
path. Other flows that share those links must be able to increase their rates rapidly to put that newly 
idle capacity to use promptly. Moreover, achieving low tail flow completion times requires effective 
multi-path spreading of flows across the fabric’s many paths. 
In oversubscribed workloads, stretching of tail completion times for “losing” flows can become a 
bottleneck for overall application performance. These performance constraints demand unprecedented 
responsiveness from congestion control protocols, and in turn call for new inputs to congestion control 
that offer fine-grained and timely information about the congestion state of the network fabric. 
End-host congestion control has long employed simple end-to-end signals, such as round-trip time (RTT) 
and packet loss events, to summarize the extent of congestion on a full path. Using explicit congestion 
notification (ECN) marks, switches directly provide a simple, Boolean indication to end hosts of whether, 
short of a drop, a packet encounters more than some threshold queue length at one or more hops along 
its path. These established signals are coarse grained: as end-to-end, path-summary metrics, RTT, and 
packet-loss event counts reflect the total congestion along a full path and do not reveal conditions at 
any individual switch. 
An ECN mark offers only a single-bit quantification of congestion, and as a path summary, reports only 
the presence of congestion along any extent of the full path, or its absence along all of it. These signals 
do not meet the finer-grained congestion control needs of contemporary ML and HPC workloads. For 
example, to increase a flow’s sending rate rapidly to match the abruptly released capacity on a link 
precisely, a sender would benefit from discovering the available bandwidth at the single most 
constrained link along a path: the link with the minimum available bandwidth. 
While per-link available bandwidth is readily measurable by individual switches, it is not directly 
observable by end hosts. Absent finer-grained signals, status-quo congestion control approaches are 
limited to searching conservatively for a path’s available bandwidth by increasing their sending rate and 
detecting when they congest the path—a slow process. 
As another example, end-to-end delay measurements mix together queueing delays from all congested 
hops along a path, such that in delay-based congestion control, the number of congested hops 
determines a sender’s perceived extent of congestion, which can yield bandwidth unfairness between 
flows. Only the queueing delay at a flow’s single most congested hop (its true bottleneck) should 
determine a sender’s sending rate. This quantity, again, is not directly observable by end hosts using 
today’s coarse-grained, path-summary signals.
